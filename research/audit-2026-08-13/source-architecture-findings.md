- **[measured]** Only two published fields are lost outright by going spreadsheet-primary: `category` and `award_type`. All eight others have a direct or near-direct column.
  - *Why it matters:* The field-fidelity objection to the spreadsheets is far narrower than 'we lose the PDF schema'. It is two fields, and one of them is not a real field.
- **[measured]** `category` is not recoverable by joining the spreadsheet `source` to the PDF-derived initiatives file, because that join table is internally inconsistent with the repo's own awards file in 11 of the 13 years that have both.
  - *Why it matters:* Any plan that says 'category is derivable by join' is currently false for every year except FY2015 and FY2027. It is also a live defect in data the repo publishes today, independent of the spreadsheet question.
- **[measured]** The defective side of that contradiction is the initiatives file's category column, not the awards file, and for at least four years the defect is a one-block heading lag.
  - *Why it matters:* It localizes the bug to the initiatives extraction rather than the awards extraction, and it means `category` becomes join-derivable once the initiatives file is repaired — so this loss is fixable, not structural. FY2017 and FY2018 are not explained by any shift and need separate work.
- **[measured]** `award_type` is not a Schedule C field at all; it is a derived flag over `member`, and the spreadsheet reproduces it at 86.8%–89.6%, not 100%.
  - *Why it matters:* 'award_type is lost' overstates it — the flag can be recomputed the same way on the spreadsheet's own member column. But it will not be the same partition: FY2027 alone has 383 rows the PDF calls initiative_provider where the spreadsheet names a member.
- **[measured]** The FY2016-only award_type test is a test that cannot fail and must not be cited.
  - *Why it matters:* This is precisely the single-year trap in the cautionary record. A reviewer running FY2016 alone would conclude award_type is trivially derivable and be wrong by ~13 points.
- **[measured]** `member` survives but changes meaning in two distinct, separately-sized ways — and roster drift is only one of them.
  - *Why it matters:* The standing rule already bars member from the join key. What is new: even as a display field, the spreadsheet silently replaces the sponsoring member with 'Speaker' on Speaker's-Initiative rows in both FY2024 and FY2027 — a systematic attribution loss that has nothing to do with the roster and would not be fixed by pinning the roster.
- **[measured]** The spreadsheet is better filled than the published PDF corpus on every surviving field in every year measured, in several cases by an order of magnitude.
  - *Why it matters:* The field-fidelity question was posed as what is lost. On seven of the eight surviving fields the honest answer is that coverage goes up, not down.
- **[measured]** Where both sources populate the same field, they agree — so the spreadsheet's extra fill is additional data, not competing data.
  - *Why it matters:* Removes the main risk of a spreadsheet-primary corpus contradicting what the repo already published on shared fields. The disagreement rate is 0.05%–3.6% depending on field and year, not a systematic offset.

My adjudication that the initiatives file is the defective side rests entirely on repo-internal evidence: cross-year agreement of the same initiative name in FY2015/FY2024/FY2027 against FY2020, plus the initiatives file's own `agencies` column (DCLA rows labeled 'Criminal Justice Services'). That is Council-published data, but it is inference from consistency, not a read of the printed heading. Someone should confirm against the FY2020 PDF before the initiatives file is edited.

2) Why FY2017 and FY2018 fail the consistency test under every shift I tried (best recovery 64/295 and 34/263). Whatever is wrong there is not a uniform block lag and I did not diagnose it.

3) member fidelity for FY2016 — the PDF member column is 0% filled, so there is nothing to compare. Same for award_type: the FY2016 test is degenerate and I have reported it as non-evidence rather than as a 99.6% result.

## 2. The hypothesis fails on dollars. Transparency resolutions are a TRANSFER ledger, not new money: gross designations are near-exactly cancelled by paired rescissions, so TR net dollars are under 10% of the spreadsheet-minus-ScheduleC gap in all 12 measurable years and NEGATIVE in 3 (FY2014, FY2016, FY2026). - **[measured]** Transparency resolutions are a transfer ledger, not a source of net new designations. Gross positive designations are cancelled by paired rescissions to within a few percent, in every one of the 16 parsed years.
  - *Why it matters:* The hypothesis treats TR as post-adoption money that would close the spreadsheet gap. Mechanically it is not new money -- it is the Council moving already-adopted dollars between recipients. A union of Schedule C and TR cannot add dollars TR never added.
- **[measured]** TR cannot account for the FY2016 gap, or any year's gap. Direct answer to the thread question: of the $291,459,614 FY2016 gap between the $381,376,626 spreadsheet and the $89,917,012 extracted Schedule C, TR net accounts for -$1,400,743 = -0.5%. It widens the gap.
  - *Why it matters:* Retires the framing that the repo 'already holds the complete picture and merely never unions it.' The union was computed and it does not close. Whatever the missing $200-290M is, TR is not where it lives.
- **[measured]** TR is nonetheless NOT a rounding error. On identity -- which organization a dollar ended up at -- it is the single largest contributor the repo has for FY2015-FY2023, roughly doubling how much of the spreadsheet can be matched at all.
  - *Why it matters:* The two claims are not in tension and must ship together. Anyone reading only 'TR net is -0.5%' would wrongly conclude the TR corpus is disposable. It carries recipient identity for thousands of awards that exist nowhere else in the repo's parsed PDF data.
- **[measured]** The union still fails on its own terms. Schedule C plus TR together leave 11.8%-57.9% of spreadsheet dollars and 32.1%-68.2% of spreadsheet rows unmatched, in every year without exception.
  - *Why it matters:* Answers the hypothesis negatively for the whole corpus, not one year. Note this measurement is a LOWER bound on true overlap (see could_not_check), so 'the union fails' is safe while 'the union covers exactly X%' is not.
- **[measured]** The $291M FY2016 framing measures an extraction failure, not a universe difference. Against the Schedule C INITIATIVE envelope the gap is $48,190,052, not $291,459,614.
  - *Why it matters:* Reframes the thread. About 83% of the FY2016 'gap' is award rows the parser never extracted from a PDF that does contain the envelope; only ~$48M is genuine post-adoption headroom. TR net (-$1.4M) still fails to explain even that $48M, but the remediation target is the award parser, not a new data source.
- **[measured]** FY2025 transparency resolutions are obtained but entirely unparsed. FY2027 was never obtained, and likely does not exist yet.
  - *Why it matters:* FY2025 is a pure parsing backlog worth roughly 4,000-5,000 designation rows at the corpus average -- the cheapest coverage win available, and it explains why FY2025's union dollar coverage (68.6%) sits below FY2024's (83.4%) and FY2026's (88.2%). FY2027 needs an acquisition step, not a parse. The 'does not exist yet' half is inferred from the in-repo date pattern only; I did not query council.nyc.gov.
- **[measured]** Two further gaps sit inside years that currently look complete, and no reconciliation file names them as failures.
  - *Why it matters:* Published FY2010-FY2013 TR totals are understated by an unknown amount concentrated in 234 unmined pages, so any per-year TR dollar figure for those years must carry that caveat. This is also the natural consumer of the FY2009 OCR pipeline noted in the workspace change log.
- **[measured]** For FY2010-FY2014 the transparency resolutions are the ONLY organization-level award data the repo holds.
  - *Why it matters:* For five fiscal years the answer to 'is TR a rounding error' is that it is 100% of what exists. Deprioritizing the TR corpus would delete the repo's only recipient-level coverage of FY2010-FY2014, and FY2010-FY2013 have no spreadsheet to fall back on.

**Could not check:** 1) Whether FY2027 transparency resolutions have been published by the Council. That needs an external fetch of council.nyc.gov, which was not authorized for this read-only task. - **[measured]** The FY2027 62 rows are NOT transparency-resolution designations. They are undesignated agency POOL placeholders -- the thing a transparency resolution later consumes, not the thing it produces.
  - *Why it matters:* The thread asked whether those 62 rows are in fact transparency-resolution designations. The answer is no, and the distinction is load-bearing: a TR designation is a named provider receiving money, while these are the unassigned pool sitting with the administering agency. Writing 'the FY2027 gap is transparency resolutions' would be wrong in the same shape as calling an empty envelope a letter. The correct statement is that the gap is money awaiting the FY2027 transparency resolutions, which have not been written.
- **[measured]** No FY2027 transparency resolution exists -- not in this repo, and per the repo's own live check, not in the world as of 2026-07-07.
  - *Why it matters:* FY2027 cannot be used to test the union hypothesis at all, in either direction. It also sets an expectation with a date on it: every observed series starts in the second half of August (FY2024 Aug 3, FY2025 Aug 15, FY2026 Aug 14). Today is 2026-08-13. The first FY2027 transparency resolution is due within days, and when it lands the FY2027 gap should begin converting from agency-pool rows into named-provider rows.
- **[measured]** For FY2026 the hypothesis holds end to end: Schedule C plus its transparency resolutions accounts for the disclosure workbook, and the workbook is a dated cumulative record of both.
  - *Why it matters:* This is the conclusion the thread was built to reach, and it is a very different recommendation from 'switch to the spreadsheets.' For this year the repo already holds the complete picture in primary PDF form and simply never unions the two datasets. The fix is a join, not a source migration.
- **[measured]** The 87% match rate survives a null test that could have killed it. The join is year-specific, not an artifact of coincidental (EIN, amount) collisions.
  - *Why it matters:* The cautionary record says a test that cannot fail is worse than no test. An 87% match on a two-field key across corpora of thousands of rows is precisely the kind of number that could be pure collision. It is not, and now that is on the record rather than assumed. It also shows the row-percentage is the softer metric and the dollar-percentage the harder one.
- **[measured]** The FY2026 workbook is a snapshot dated 2026-06-08, and the transparency resolution adopted 22 days later leaves no trace in it.
  - *Why it matters:* This is the strongest single result in the thread because it is a genuine falsifier: had reso 10 shown up at the same rate as resos 1-9, the cumulative-record story would have been in trouble. It also means every disclosure workbook must be read as of its export date, not as of its fiscal year. The FY2024, FY2025 and FY2026 workbooks were all exported in the same 2026-06-08 session; FY2027 on 2026-07-01, one day into its fiscal year, which is why FY2027 shows the highest Schedule-C-alone coverage of any year (99.4% of rows) -- nothing had happened yet.
- **[measured]** The hypothesis FAILS for FY2016 through FY2020, and the cause is Schedule C extraction, not the transparency resolutions.
  - *Why it matters:* Directly binding on the cautionary record: two conclusions were published today from a single fiscal year and both were wrong. 'Schedule C plus TR equals the disclosure spreadsheet' is true for FY2021-FY2024 and FY2026 and false for FY2016-FY2020. The corpus-wide 46.4% row / 56.1% dollar figure that motivated the whole thread is dominated by the early years, where the union does not rescue the shortfall because the Schedule C side was barely extracted. Any recommendation must be year-scoped.
- **[measured]** A THIRD in-repo source is required and already exists: the Schedule C initiatives file, which carries lump-sum initiatives that have no provider table.
  - *Why it matters:* The union is not two datasets but three: Schedule C awards, Schedule C initiatives, and transparency resolutions. Omitting the initiatives file leaves a single $15.75M row unexplained and inflates the apparent residual. Whoever implements the union needs to know this before measuring their own coverage.
- **[measured]** Most of the FY2026 residual on both sides is the same awards with revised amounts, not missing awards.
